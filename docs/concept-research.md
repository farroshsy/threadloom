# Research Catalog: Personal Software That Can Become a Life Artifact

## Research framing

The supplied brief correctly rejects the familiar response to personal friction: add another inbox, reminder surface, dashboard, tracker, or assistant. Personal-information research instead points to a more specific problem: information becomes fragmented, loses context, and is often difficult to retrieve when it matters.[1] Lifelogging research shows that personal data can support memory and reflection, but also makes clear that indiscriminate capture creates an archive rather than a reason to return.[2] Sellen and Whittaker’s critique is especially useful: useful personal systems should declare whether they support recollection, reminiscence, retrieval, reflection, or future intention—and then design meaningful cues for that purpose.[3]

The catalog therefore favors concepts with a **deliberate capture boundary**, an evolving physical-world metaphor, and a return mechanism that is neither a notification nor a productivity score. Scores are hypotheses for prioritization, not market-size claims.

## Score key

Each idea is scored from **1 (low)** to **5 (high)** on **O**riginality, **B**eauty, **L**ongevity, **P**ersonal usefulness, **D**aily usefulness, **E**motional attachment, **F**un, e**X**pandability, **T**echnical depth, and **M**emorability. The final number is the total out of 50.

## The ten strongest directions

### 1. Threadloom — selected

**Product vision and core loop.** Threadloom is a local-first personal context loom: a place for loose ends that are too meaningful to become tasks and too unfinished to become notes. A person captures a strand in seconds, including why it matters and, optionally, the cue that should invite a return. On a later visit, they pull a strand into focus and either deepen it, let it rest, connect it, resolve it, or deliberately release it. The intended user journey begins with a few real open loops, becomes a weekly reflective ritual, and eventually becomes a textured artifact of questions, people, places, and changes.

**Interaction, visual world, and information architecture.** The product is a textile archive rather than a dashboard. The primary surface is the loom; a margin inspector explains the selected strand; a compact return rail proposes a few quiet revisits. Context tags and state form the first information architecture, followed later by people, place, era, and “why now?” views. Thickness, fading, knots, and color express age and relationship. The world-building stays grounded: the loom is a metaphor for holding and connecting, not a game mechanic.

**Progression, stack, risks, MVP, and evolution.** Progression is visual and historical rather than score-based. The MVP requires typed strands, local persistence, a compelling loom, capture, state transitions, clear sample-data labeling, and export planning. React, TypeScript, IndexedDB, and Canvas/SVG provide an appropriate client-first stack; AI is strictly optional for local clustering after the manual model proves useful. The main risk is beauty without return value, so failure is measured by whether users cannot answer “why should I open this today?” Future evolution is encrypted export/import, user-controlled resurfacing logic, relationship-aware views, and optional end-to-end encrypted sync.

### 2. The Last Place

**Product vision and core loop.** The Last Place solves a familiar but poorly served question: “Where did I actually use that?” Instead of organizing files, links, objects, and facts by container, it records their last meaningful use: preparing a meal, helping a friend, solving a bug, sitting in a certain room. The user makes a brief last-use mark at the moment of relevance, then returns through human context rather than a filename.

**Interaction, visual world, and information architecture.** Objects live in small dioramas or footprints rather than folders. A retrieval query starts with place, person, event, or purpose, and the interface shows the lived occasion around the item. The information architecture links items to episodes, people, places, and sources. Its visual world is an “as-lived index”: worn surfaces, contextual traces, and paths back to usefulness.

**Progression, stack, risks, MVP, and evolution.** The archive becomes more valuable as episodes accumulate and the number of successful re-finds rises. A browser extension and file-picker integration would ultimately make capture practical, but the MVP must begin with links and manual object marks. Technical depth is high: browser history or desktop integration, local search, privacy controls, and cross-source indexing. It fails if capture interrupts life or if search still feels slower than re-Googling; the first test is whether a user can re-find ten real items faster through context than through folders.

### 3. Untitled Folder

**Product vision and core loop.** Untitled Folder protects the state before an idea, file, reference, or thought has a category. Users drop fragments into an “unfiled tray” with only the context of encounter. They return to browse relationships that emerge naturally, choosing later to name, combine, archive, or dissolve a fragment. This solves the false binary between careful organization and information loss.

**Interaction, visual world, and information architecture.** The interface resembles an archival worktable with provisional labels, string, translucent paper, and material groupings. Information architecture begins with fragments and provenance, then grows relationships through shared context, manual links, and user-approved suggestions. The central interaction is not filing; it is revisiting adjacent fragments and deciding whether a relation is real.

**Progression, stack, risks, MVP, and evolution.** The archive becomes visually richer as ambiguous fragments gain history. The MVP includes text, links, source/time, a tray, manual links, search, and unambiguous deletion; a local embedding index is a later enhancement, not a prerequisite. The risk is becoming an elegant dumping ground, so the return loop must surface small related groups, never a giant inbox. It succeeds if it produces unexpected, useful connections without demanding a taxonomy.

### 4. Commonplace of Repairs

**Product vision and core loop.** Commonplace of Repairs is a private, cross-domain repair manual for the user’s own life. It records symptoms, attempts, tools, outcomes, and lessons for material repairs, difficult conversations, technical recovery, creative stuckness, and personal resets. The core loop is: notice a rupture, document the repair attempt, then revise the recipe after living with the result.

**Interaction, visual world, and information architecture.** A workshop-and-mending visual language gives each repair a material quality: seam, patch, splice, polish, or reassembly. Users enter through a symptom or past repair, then navigate by tool, pattern, domain, and confidence. The world builds a personal repair bench rather than a troubleshooting database.

**Progression, stack, risks, MVP, and evolution.** Repeated repairs become dependable techniques, while unsuccessful attempts remain useful evidence. The MVP is a typed repair record, optional photo, pattern filters, and a “what worked last time?” retrieval mode. A local-first PWA with full-text search is sufficient; visual similarity and AI-assisted recipe extraction can follow. The ethical risk is collapsing relationships into procedures, so the system must preserve uncertainty and never prescribe behavior as if it were authoritative.

### 5. Attention Residue

**Product vision and core loop.** Attention Residue gives people a place to offload the mental fragments left after meetings, media, errands, or conversations before those fragments turn into diffuse background load. The user captures a one-line residue, decides whether it is signal, a strand, an action, or release, and revisits clusters that keep returning. It explicitly solves the gap between fleeting attention and overbuilt note systems.

**Interaction, visual world, and information architecture.** The visual metaphor is a set of settling trays and light particles, not a feed. Residue can become a Threadloom-like strand, but the product’s distinctive view is the accumulation and release of fragments. Information architecture centers source, emotional tone, salience, and disposition. A system may suggest groupings, but the user makes the meaningful classification.

**Progression, stack, risks, MVP, and evolution.** Mature use reveals which sources generate residue worth keeping and which create noise. The MVP is a frictionless capture surface, four dispositions, a week view, and a release gesture. Browser and calendar integrations are tempting but should be deferred until manual capture establishes trust. It fails if it becomes another inbox; it succeeds if users feel mentally lighter after three minutes without creating new obligations.

### 6. Undertow

**Product vision and core loop.** Undertow reveals themes that quietly pull attention across disparate moments: a recurring desire for repair, belonging, solitude, movement, or making. Users add only sparse personal observations; the system periodically offers a possible thematic current with the evidence visible. The core loop is noticing, naming or declining a pattern, and seeing how it changes through seasons.

**Interaction, visual world, and information architecture.** The world is a calm current field in which a user can inspect the visible fragments that bend a flow. This deliberately avoids claiming psychological truth. Information architecture uses observations, themes, confidence, and user feedback. The central interaction is an evidence-first pattern card: “These five moments may be connected—does that feel true?”

**Progression, stack, risks, MVP, and evolution.** The visual landscape becomes better after months, not days. The MVP can work with manual themes and links; privacy-preserving local embeddings are a later upgrade. It has high technical and ethical risk: opaque clustering may feel invasive, overconfident, or wrong. The failure mode is a vague “AI insight” machine; the mitigation is visible evidence, opt-in suggestions, the ability to reject patterns, and no diagnostic language.

### 7. Refrain

**Product vision and core loop.** Refrain records phrases, questions, images, and concerns that recur across a person’s life. The user adds a fragment when it catches them, then returns to see variations rather than counts. The value is recognizing a personal motif—what keeps singing underneath the noise—without making identity feel fixed.

**Interaction, visual world, and information architecture.** The visual style is typographic and musical: phrases return as lines in a score, with small changes in emphasis, source, and era. Fragments may connect to people, books, places, and projects. The information architecture has fragments, refrains, sources, versions, and intervals; it should privilege textual provenance over automatic sentiment labels.

**Progression, stack, risks, MVP, and evolution.** Over years, refrains become a distinctive autobiography of language. The MVP needs capture, full-text retrieval, manual grouping, a chronological score, and a view that shows all variations of one phrase. Local semantic search is a promising upgrade, but entirely manual grouping validates the model. It fails if every repeated word is treated as meaningful; the user must choose what becomes a refrain.

### 8. The Holding Pattern

**Product vision and core loop.** The Holding Pattern gives form to prolonged waiting: applications, diagnoses, recoveries, repairs, responses, or plans that cannot yet become action. Users create a suspended item, record what would count as a signal, document the surrounding life, and periodically decide whether the circle should continue, widen, or end.

**Interaction, visual world, and information architecture.** Each item is a soft looping route, not a countdown. The interface distinguishes waiting for a specific signal from living inside uncertainty. Information architecture includes the awaited signal, practical contingency, emotional note, next review condition, and related people or documents. The world-building converts empty time into a legible season without promising control.

**Progression, stack, risks, MVP, and evolution.** The archive becomes useful when a person can see how long waits actually unfolded and how they coped. The MVP contains manual items, state revisions, a “during the wait” log, and gentle user-opened reviews. Optional inbox or status-page integrations may follow. It fails if it intensifies anxious checking, so automatic push notifications should be absent until a user explicitly asks for them.

### 9. The Eavesdropper’s Library

**Product vision and core loop.** The Eavesdropper’s Library is an archive of questions one encounters—in conversation, books, dreams, work, or public life—before the pressure to answer them arrives. A user captures a question and its encounter context, lets it remain open, and returns through recurring questions or surprising adjacent shelves. It solves the loss of curiosity in systems optimized for answers.

**Interaction, visual world, and information architecture.** Questions become physical book spines and marginal cards; shelf arrangement changes by question type, source, and re-encounter rather than conventional subject taxonomy. The information architecture includes question, source, first encounter, later evidence, responses, and related questions. The central interaction is opening a question’s “margin,” where later life events can be placed beside the original wording.

**Progression, stack, risks, MVP, and evolution.** The library gains texture and intellectual identity after years. The MVP is text capture, source/provenance, full-text search, manual shelving, and a random question revisit. AI can later retrieve relevant user-owned fragments, but it should never produce an answer by default. It fails if the library becomes a quote collector; meaningful revisit prompts must privilege unfinished questions and lived evidence.

### 10. Groundswell

**Product vision and core loop.** Groundswell makes slow influence visible. Instead of crediting a new belief, taste, or decision to one dramatic source, users collect small inputs and later note when something shifted. The loop is capture an influence, add an occasional “this changed how I see…” observation, and revisit the geological story of a conviction.

**Interaction, visual world, and information architecture.** The product is an evolving cross-section: publications, conversations, places, and experiences are sediment; later insights are outcrops. Information architecture links source, influence note, topic, confidence, and later consequence. The interface emphasizes that meaning was constructed slowly, not discovered by an algorithm.

**Progression, stack, risks, MVP, and evolution.** Years create an unusually rich record of intellectual and personal formation. The MVP uses source cards, manual relationships, and a topic cross-section. Local vector similarity could later help surface likely layers. It fails if users cannot see a benefit before a year of data, so the prototype should provide immediate value through a single topic story after only six to ten inputs.

## Final selection: Threadloom

Threadloom is the best concept because it occupies an overlooked space between a note, a reminder, and a personal archive. It does not require total capture or constant behavior tracking, yet it creates a valuable record through ordinary use. It can support **retrieval, reflection, and prospective intention** while preserving the crucial piece that existing tools commonly discard: the original reason something mattered.[1][3]

### The self-critique

The question is not whether the loom is aesthetically novel; it is whether someone would genuinely miss Threadloom if it disappeared. In its first prototype form, the honest answer is **not yet**. A beautiful visualization of manually entered loose ends is insufficient. It becomes missable only if a user experiences at least one of three concrete losses without it: they cannot re-enter a dormant project without the preserved context, they lose a meaningful curiosity because it had no home, or they miss a timely reconnection to a person/place/question that the loom helped retain.

The product must therefore earn emotional attachment through the **return moment**, not through the capture moment. The upgrade path in this repository is deliberately designed around that test: typed context, state transitions, selected return set, no nagging notifications, and an architecture ready for user-controlled resurfacing. If repeated user testing does not show that the “why” makes returns appreciably more useful than a notes app, the team should stop polishing the metaphor and revise the core loop.
